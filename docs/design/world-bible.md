# World & Story Bible — *The Drowned Chorus*

Status: **canonical** as of 2026-07-30 (the **Release revision**). This tightens
the 2026-07-24 Rite canon to a single engine — *the chorus cannot take, only
receive* — from which the whole world, the rite, the villain, and the ending are
now derived rather than asserted. It supersedes the placeholder "Project
Meterfall" codename (PRD §18's naming question is resolved here: the game is **The
Drowned Chorus**).

The world is built from the recorded setlist of the band **Inhalants** — the
game's world *is* those songs, sunk and given a place. The playable character is
**Mir** alone (PRD v10.0), but the *story* is a family of three: Mir, his partner
**Lunal**, and their toddler son **Nari**. (History: an earlier draft cast four
song-title heroes; v10.0 made it solo; the Rite revision moved the inciting event
*inside* the Fold and made Lunal Nari's mother; the Release revision makes their
disagreement, and the ending, resolve on one law. Those four old names survive
only as the soundtrack's songs, §11.)

**How the story is told.** Almost none of what follows is ever spoken. The game
narrates through *place* (arena staging, §7), through *ground* (the footprint
trail, the tracking puzzle, §6.4), through ~55 found **echoes** (§8), and through
three lines at the very end (§9). This bible is the canon *beneath* all of that —
the iceberg under the waterline. When in doubt, the rule is the game's oldest one:
**found, not told.**

---

## 1. Logline

A drowned father carries his son up out of a town that has lawfully condemned the
boy, singing back on the beat against the song that wants them both — only to find
the one who took his child at the top of the world is the boy's own mother, and
that saving him will mean doing the one thing neither parent could bear: letting
him go.

## 2. Premise — the Rite, and the flight from it

At the bottom of a lightless sea lies **the Fold**, a gothic town whose people
pray to a massive **obelisk**. They did not raise it; they woke on the ocean floor
and it was already there, already listening. Time is broken here — the clocks
melt, nothing rots, nothing rests — because the whole drowned realm hums one
endless composition, **the drowned chorus**, and the obelisk is that chorus made
monument.

The Fold does not merely worship the obelisk. It is *governed* by it. **Once a
year the obelisk opens, and the litho comes out**: a small, baby-shaped stone
idol, a child's face with no breath behind it, that speaks a single new **rule**
into the way-of-living. And the Fold obeys — the way it has obeyed every rule
before it, back further than anyone can remember, until no one recalls a year
without a new commandment, or who they were before the commandments. A whole
people legislated out of their own lives, one rule a year, and grateful for it.

**Mir**, his partner **Lunal**, and their toddler son **Nari** live there, and
pray there, like everyone. Then the obelisk opens, and the litho speaks this
year's rule: *the unnamed voice is owed to the deep.* It is phrased as law,
general on its face — but the whole Fold knows at once whose small voice it means.
The rule will kill Nari.

The two parents cannot agree on how to save him, and that disagreement is the
whole story (§5). Mir chooses the only heresy left: he takes his son and **runs**
— up out of the Fold, up the drowned Shelf, across the Breach where the world
crosses the waterline, toward the surface, because up is the one direction the
rule does not point. He is not climbing toward safety. He is climbing away from a
lawful execution. And at the top of the climb, the thing that finally takes Nari
is not a monster of the surface. It is the boy's own mother, saving him her way.
The rest of the game is a father fighting up through a world he misreads, toward a
truth that will break him worse than the rule did.

## 3. The one law — the chorus cannot take, only receive

*(This is the whole cosmology. Everything downstream is a consequence of it. Never
stated outright in-game; it is the physics the images obey.)*

Long ago — or outside of time, which here is the same thing — something sang a
note that did not resolve: the first phrase of a composition with no ending.
Because the chord never lands, time cannot move forward; that is why the clocks
melt and nothing heals. The unresolved note is a hunger, and it grows the only way
it can: **it cannot seize a voice. It can only accept one that is surrendered to
it.** The whole drowned world runs on that single rule, and five things fall out
of it — the five load-bearing beams of the story:

- **Surrender is the mercy and the theft in one gesture.** Hand the song your
  grief — hand it the drowning itself — and it will soothe you; and the
  handing-over is exactly what presses you into the sound. The drowned wake in the
  Fold with no memory of going under because that memory was the first thing they
  gave. Peace bought with everything, and mistaken for peace.
- **The obelisk cannot drown the Fold. It must be fed — so it governs.** An idol
  with no hands of its own rules by making the town's hands do the giving. Each
  yearly rule is a fresh surrender the people perform *willingly*, a little more
  of their lives laid on the stone, called devotion. This is how a town sings
  itself into the strata one commandment at a time and thanks the thing that eats
  it.
- **Nari cannot be taken, only given.** He is an **unwritten voice**: a child who
  has only ever been at his parents' heel, sheltered in the oldest deep of the
  Fold, who has surrendered *nothing.* The chorus has no hold on him to pull —
  which is precisely why it will bend a rule to get him, because the one way to
  claim a voice that was never given is to make someone else give it. The rule is
  not the chorus reaching for Nari. It is the chorus reaching for the Fold's
  hands.
- **Mir resists because he refuses to give.** The one grief he will not surrender
  is his son, and an ungiven grief beats against the note and will not lie flat.
  That refusal *is* the combat: to sing back on the beat (PRD §8.3) is to refuse
  to hand the phrase over; to miss is to give it up. He can fight and climb where
  the resigned drowned cannot for one reason only — he never finished drowning,
  because he never let go.
- **The last chord can be landed by exactly one thing: a voice *released*, not
  surrendered.** Surrender feeds the note; release resolves it. Letting go and
  giving up look identical from inside the drowned dark, and that confusion is why
  the song has run forever — every soul who ever tried to end it *surrendered*
  instead, and surrender only made it louder. The ending is the first time in the
  history of the world anyone tells the two apart (§9).

### The two false answers, made into objects

The world hands the player the two wrong responses to loss as two things it meets
over and over, and they are mirror images:

- **The obelisk — surrender.** It takes a grief and *dissolves* it into the song.
  Give it away, be numbed, sink. It is also the game's **save-and-rest point**,
  scattered up all five regions as the Fold's prayer-stones (PRD §8.8), and that
  is deliberate: at every obelisk Mir lets the song take the road's weariness — the
  aches, the fear, the miles — and *clenches around the one thing he will never
  lay down.* Each save is his whole character in ten seconds: **I'll give you the
  weariness. Not him.** It is why resting is safe and repeatable but never claims
  him, and it quietly explains persistence — the song learns your shape, just
  never all of it.
- **The cage — keeping.** Lunal's answer (§5). It takes a grief and *stops it
  dead* — held forever, unchanged, never given and never let go. The obelisk numbs
  the pain by dissolving it; the cage numbs it by freezing it. Both refuse to let
  the loss *move.*

Mir's ending belongs to neither object. He will not lay Nari on the stone and he
will not leave him in the cage. He does the third thing — the one no object in the
drowned world can do — and it is the only thing that ends anything.

## 4. The rhythm is diegetic

The combat mechanic is the story. A battle is a passage of the drowned chorus;
every ability is a phrase sung into it. Hitting the beat is **refusing to give the
phrase away** — countering the song; missing lets it take the phrase. The things
Mir fights are the song made flesh, drowned voices the chorus already owns, each
one the note reaching to pull him under too.

- **Focus** — a breath held for a difficult line, spent on specials.
- **Groove** (the swelling meter) — the chorus turning in *Mir's* favour; spent in
  full on an **ultimate**, a verse loud enough to break the song.
- **Sightread** — literally *seeing the music*: a forecast of the next bars and the
  strikes riding them (PRD §8.4's forecast assist).

Grief and rhythm are the same substance here. Mir fights by refusing to miss a
beat, because that is exactly how he refuses to stop running toward his son.

## 5. The family — one doomed child, three answers

The rule condemns Nari. Everyone who loves him answers it differently, and the
tragedy is that none of the answers are villainous at the root — they are three
kinds of love, and only one of them is survivable. All three are the same refusal
to lose him; only the last one knows the difference between holding on and holding
hostage.

### Mir — love as *pursuit*

A guitarist, and a father. When the litho named his son, Mir did not dispute the
rule; he started packing a bag for the climb. He is not a chosen hero and not
especially strong — he is simply the one soul in the Fold who would rather commit
the unthinkable heresy (leave) than perform the unthinkable obedience (hand over
his boy). He can sing back where the drowned cannot because he has not finished
drowning: his refusal to let go is an unresolved chord of his own, and it will not
lie flat against the world's. His kit (light / heavy / special / ultimate / dash /
parry, PRD §8.2) is verses sung against the chorus; his ultimate is the loudest a
single voice can get before the song drowns it. He runs, and fights, and climbs,
and he is *wrong about almost everything* — about what the surface is, about what
took his son — and he is right about the only thing that will matter, which is
that you do not surrender the boy. What he has to learn, at the very top, is that
*release* is not surrender — that the love that saves Nari is not the grip he has
climbed the whole world on.

### Lunal — love as *keeping*

Nari's mother, Mir's partner. She did not dispute the rule either — she only asked
where a thing could be hidden that the song could not find. Where Mir believes in
running, Lunal believes running is a lie, and she is not wrong: *you cannot
outclimb the obelisk, and the surface is not freedom — it is only a wider place to
die.* The Scar will prove her right about that. So she chose the one answer she
thought could actually work — **to keep him.** To carry the boy somewhere the rule
and the song can never reach and hold him there, exactly as he is, forever: never
given to the deep, never grown, never changed, never lost. In her logic the cage
is *mercy,* and it is the logic of a mother under a death-sentence with a
deadline, who could not win the argument in time. *"She doesn't hunt to kill. She
hunts to keep."*

At the Breach, while Mir's back is turned for a single measure, **she is the one
who takes Nari** — and the boy does not cry out, because the hands that lift him
are his mother's. She carries him up and away to the Stage and builds him a cage a
mother builds: padded bars, no door on the inside. She is the antagonist, and she
is *right that the Fold would have killed him and the surface will too.* That is
what makes her unbearable, and it is why she is never a monster: she is the
strongest version of the thing Mir is doing, and the thing he could still become.
Her horror is the horror of preservation — **love as taxidermy** — and the game
grants her the dignity of being correct about everything except what love is
allowed to cost.

*(Reveal: the player reads the truth in the ground across the Scar; Mir himself
does not know until the threshold of the Stage, §6.4–6.5. She is revealed in the
staging at the finale, and the mask she sets down is the image — PRD §8.7. A
playable Lunal confrontation is the highest-value story build remaining, §10.)*

### The Conductor — keeping, grown terminal

The boss at the far end of the world (PRD §8.7): gaunt, black-coated, a clock
where a heart should tick, a baton raised over a drowned orchestra. He is what
Lunal will become, and what Mir is one refusal away from — **a keeper so old he
has fused to the thing he was keeping.** Ages ago he too stood where Mir stands
and chose to hold on; he refused to let the song end because ending meant losing
what he held, and he held on so long that holding hollowed him into the song's own
hand. He does not want the chord to land — *landing it unmakes him* — so he
conducts to survive, and because conducting is now all that is left of him. His
endless rehearsal is the machine that keeps the drowned song stable, and Lunal did
not defeat him: she *recognized* him — her own future — and took up behind him,
and he let her, because a keeper always knows another. His three phases are the
song straining under its own weight: a stable **4/4** that fractures into **3/4**,
then a desperate **5/4 → 7/8 → 4/4 → 3/4** as the composition comes apart. The
music falters every time the small one in the cage cries — the one unwritten voice
the song cannot digest — then Lunal winds him tighter and it starts again,
angrier. To fight through the Conductor is to fight the end-state of *keep,* and
to break Lunal's wall. When it breaks, she has nothing left to hide behind.

### Nari — the unwritten voice

Mir and Lunal's toddler son, and the one thing in the deep the chorus does not own
(§3). He is never an escort objective, an inventory item, or a fail state (PRD
§8.4) — he is the *reason,* rendered entirely in the world: a small follower at
Mir's heel through the Fold and the climb, then a footprint trail that ends at a
scuffle on the surface, then sparse clues, then a cage. He is the fulcrum every
force in the world is bending toward, and being loved by these three, in this
world, is its own kind of doom. In the cage his mother built to save him, safe and
whole and unable to leave, he cries — and that crying, the sound of a kept child,
is the one note that can end the song.

## 6. The Ascent — regions as movements, and as the shape of the flight

The campaign graph (`opening_biome.json`) is one continuous climb from the
seafloor to the Stage: **20 fight-nodes** across five regions, and the road tours
the whole world before it arrives. Each region is a movement of the chorus and a
stage of the flight. Combat begins only once Mir leaves the Fold; the generator
*asserts* no fight lands inside the sanctuary — because the Fold's violence is a
*law,* not a brawl, and the fighting only starts once Mir breaks it.

| Region | Movement | What it is | Staged as | Foe |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **The Fold** | *the rule* | home, and the law that condemns the boy | the drowned harvest village + the obelisk rite | *none — Nari still follows* |
| **The Kelp Shelf** | *the flight* | the climb away from the rule, child in tow | the drowned salt mine | salt-wracked drifters |
| **The Breach** | *the taking* | the waterline — where Lunal takes Nari | the drowned carnival | drifter packs |
| **The Scar** | *the wrong search* | the hostile surface; Mir hunts a "monster" | the collector's den | elite wraiths |
| **The Conductor's Stage** | *the truth* | the mother's fortress and the cage | the drowned concert hall | **the Conductor** (Lunal behind him) |

### 6.1 The Fold — the rule

Mir wakes — has always woken — on the silt among people who pray to the obelisk
and live by its yearly rules. This is the one region with no combat: the Fold's
horror is bureaucratic, not martial. The obelisk opens (the plaza is built around
it; it doubles as the game's first save-obelisk and the site of the threshold
beat). The litho comes out and speaks: *the unnamed voice is owed to the deep.*
And then the quiet catastrophe of two parents in a house, one packing a bag and
one testing locks, the child asleep between them — the same love splitting into
its two survivable-looking shapes, run and keep, neither of them yet knowing both
are wrong. Mir's first step out fires the threshold beat once (*"THE FOLD BEHIND
YOU / The chorus begins."*): the moment he stops being a worshipper and becomes a
fugitive.

### 6.2 The Kelp Shelf — the flight

The drowned climb: ship skeletons standing like a dead forest, every wreck that
ever sank pointing the same way, *up.* Mir carries or leads Nari through it — the
boy's small prints still beside his on the masts (the follower is still with him
here; treasure it, it does not last). This is the region of bargains with weight
and gravity, of *"rope enough to reach the light if you don't weigh anything
anymore"* — surrender dressed up as ascent — and Mir climbs it badly, because he
is carrying the one weight he will never set down. Its people are the miners who
listened to a singing gallery until they turned to salt: the Fold's fate in
miniature, a whole crew that *surrendered by degrees*, and a warning Mir is too
frightened to hear.

### 6.3 The Breach — the taking

The waterline: the one seam in the world where the drowned dark meets the air,
thin as a footstep — thin enough that a small thing can slip through it, or be
carried through it. Staged as a carnival that kept performing after the water came.
**This is where Nari is taken, and it is Lunal who takes him.** On Mir's first step
onto the surface the loss beat fires once (*"NARI? / He was right behind you."*):
Mir turns for a single measure and the boy is simply gone, the follower sprite
fading, the fact persisted forever on the save. Mir reads it as an ambush by the
surface. The ground tells the truer story to anyone who studies it — the scuffle
is two feet, then one, then none; there is no blood; a second, adult set of prints
reaches the surface *ahead* of theirs, and the small ones go with them, unhurried.
The boy did not cry out. Everything after the Breach is *after* the taking, and Mir
spends all of it hunting the wrong culprit.

### 6.4 The Scar — the wrong search *(the long middle: 11 nodes)*

The hostile surface, and the heart of the game — a huge ruined monster-strewn waste
of claw gouges and scorch, where *"the surface does not want you."* The Scar's
length is the point: despair is long, and a father searching for a stolen child
will walk a hundred wrong miles. It also quietly proves Lunal right — the surface
is not freedom, only a wider grave — even as it hides that she is the one he's
chasing. Mir believes a beast took his son to a den, and the surface obliges the
belief with horrors. The search runs as a real progression in the ground (the
v12.2 tracking mystery — Nari's gait is learnable: paired stride, a heel dot, a
faint right-foot drag):

- **The Fresh Trail** *(early Scar).* Prints still clear, Mir close behind. Hope
  with teeth.
- **The False Trails** *(mid Scar).* Pilgrims' strides, three-toed den-things,
  single-file decoys that double back. He loses the trail and finds it and loses it
  — *"they walk IN. Toward the den. Why would he walk toward it?"* And the ground
  begins to rot his monster-story: no blood, prints set down *gently* one at a
  time, a woman's stride always beside the small ones, *leading, not chasing.*
- **The Den's Mouth** *(late Scar).* The tracks all lead one way and none lead
  back. A collector's shelf: little things arranged by size, kept, not eaten. This
  is where the player understands what Mir still cannot: the keeper is not a beast.
  The keeper is careful. The keeper is a person who loves him.

**The Oasis** *(a secret, off the road).* One impossible thing survives in the dead
surface: a small pocket of *living* green — warm moss, a clear spring at its heart
— the single place the drowning never reached. It rhymes with Nari: he is the
**unwritten voice**, the one thing the song has not claimed, and the oasis is the
one ground the song has not drowned. It is never on the critical path; a player has
to leave the road to find it. Finding it is finding, in miniature, the exact thing
Mir is climbing to protect — and the exact thing Lunal means to cage: proof that
one live thing can persist in a world built to sink everything, and proof of how
badly you would want to keep it.

*(Design note: the 11 Scar nodes currently draw one fight pool, and the Scar is one
arena kit — so the long back half plays as one biome. The painted district zones
(bone fields, sulfur barrens, scorchreach, salt pan, basalt flats, the oasis…) make
*crossing* it read as varied land; giving those sub-stretches distinct fights/foes,
and spreading more of the 20 nodes into the other biomes, is the campaign-side fix
— §10.)*

### 6.5 The Conductor's Stage — the truth

A drowned concert plaza and the hall beyond it. At its threshold, the ground stops
lying to Mir: something only Lunal would leave — a token, the padded bars visible
through the doors, the stride he has slept beside for years — and he *knows,*
finally, that the "monster" is the boy's mother, and has been since the Breach. He
walks into the last room already broken by it. Inside is the cage, the Conductor at
his stand, and behind the baton, Lunal — who did not battle her way to the Stage
but *bargained* her way to it, giving the Conductor a reason to keep playing and
taking his strings in exchange, so that his endless rehearsal walls off the one
room in the world the rule cannot enter.

The Stage poses the game's real question as a fight, and Mir now knows exactly whom
he is answering. Lunal is offering him *her* answer, made real — your son, kept,
whole, safe, forever, in the only place the song can't touch — and she is offering
it as the woman he loves, who is right that every other door leads to the boy's
death. To win is not to prove her wrong. It is to break the wall of *keeping* (the
Conductor), reach the cage, and choose the one answer neither of them could reach
while they were still refusing to lose him: to let the last chord land, knowing it
ends the stillness that keeps the boy exactly as he is. That choice is the ending —
and it is not a father overruling a mother. It is two people who spent an entire
drowned world refusing to let go, by opposite means, finally learning together how.

## 7. The untold stories (canonical arena staging)

Each movement is fought inside a place staged to imply a past the game never
narrates (PRD §11.1.1). Never surfaced as text in-game; it exists so the art stays
coherent.

- **The Fold (the drowned village + the rite).** The village drowned during its
  harvest festival — the ring of boats was a blessing rite for the fleet; the water
  came *with* the first verse, and most didn't run. Now the survivors pray to the
  obelisk they woke to, and once a year it opens and the stone child legislates. One
  boat still strains at its mooring: the baker's daughter cut everyone else's loose
  first, and hers ran out of rope — the Fold's whole ethic in one image,
  self-sacrifice curdled into obedience. Mir is the first in living memory to refuse
  the rule and leave.
- **The Kelp Shelf (the salt mine).** Miners broke into a gallery that sang back and
  stayed to listen; turning to salt is what surrendering to it too long *is,* down
  here. The one statue facing away is the foreman, who covered his ears and ran, and
  calcified anyway, mid-stride, three steps from the lift.
- **The Breach (the carnival).** A carnival that kept performing after the water
  came. The champion's final match was against something that came up through the
  ring floor; the ropes snapped outward when it left with him. This is the
  waterline, the seam thin enough that a small thing is carried through. The tipped
  chairs face away because in the end no one could watch.
- **The Scar (the collector's den).** The tracks all lead one way, *in,* and none
  lead back. Whatever keeps this place collects rather than kills, arranged by size.
  Mir reads it as a monster's larder; the staging quietly contradicts him — the
  care, the arrangement, the gentleness of it — because it was never a beast. (An
  older tenant is staged here too: a room boarded from the inside, where someone
  locked themselves in to transcribe the sound before it finished transcribing
  them — a keeper of a smaller, sadder kind, and a rhyme for the Conductor.)
- **The Conductor's Stage (the concert hall).** The orchestra drowned rehearsing the
  ending. The Conductor kept conducting; the stands' pages are blank because he
  erases every attempt but the last row's, the only players who ever got it right
  and whom he could never let leave. The clocks each stopped at the moment a player
  gave up. The music falters every time the small one in the cage cries. Behind the
  baton, a mother watches, and keeps.

## 8. The echoes — the explorable world (PRD §8.8)

Strewn off the critical path are **~55 echoes** (11 per region) — hand-placed lore
fragments, each surfacing one line when found (PRD §8.8.2). No echo text is ever
shown automatically; the player must walk to it and interact. They carry the
braided threads of the Rite story, and — from the Shelf up — the slow correction of
Mir's mistake:

- **The Fold** plants the whole engine of the plot before Mir ever leaves: the
  yearly rite (*"Once a year the obelisk opens and the stone child speaks a new
  rule. Once a year we obey."*), the litho (*"A baby's face, and no breath behind
  it."*), the rule (*"The unnamed voice is owed to the deep. We all knew whose small
  voice it meant."*), the law beneath it (*"It has no hands. That is why it needs
  ours."*), and the split (*"One of us wanted to run. One of us wanted to hide him.
  He slept between us."*).
- **The Kelp Shelf** is the flight and its warning: *"We aren't climbing toward
  anything. We're climbing away from a rule."* — *"The miners gave the gallery a
  little of themselves to hear it better. That is all salt is."* — *"Little prints
  on the mast beside mine. He was still with me here."*
- **The Breach** is the taking, seen wrong and seen true: *"The scuffle is two feet,
  then one, then none."* — *"A second set of prints reached the air before ours."* —
  *"Whatever lifted him did not frighten him. He did not cry out. That is the part I
  cannot forgive."*
- **The Scar** is the wrong search rotting into dread: *"It doesn't eat what it
  takes. It collects."* — *"Beside the small ones, always: a woman's stride. Not
  chasing. Leading."* — *"The surface was never going to save him. She was right
  about that. I have walked far enough now to know it."*
- **The Stage** is the truth, and the law that resolves it: *"She doesn't hunt to
  kill. She hunts to keep."* — *"A mother built it. That is why the bars are padded,
  and there is no door on the inside."* — *"The stone takes what you give it. The
  cage keeps what you won't. Only one thing left to try, and it is the hardest: let
  him go."*

**Secrets (PRD §8.8.3).** Each region hides at least one non-obvious traversal
element gating a relic or echo, never critical-path content — hand-placed, because
a discoverable secret has to be a decision someone made.

## 9. The ending

Mir breaks the Conductor — the wall of *keeping* — and the baton comes free of
Lunal's hand. And now the choice, made by a father who knows exactly who is asking:
his son, in a cage his wife built, safe and whole and crying; a mother beside it
who is right that every road out of here is a grave; and one thing neither of them
has tried, because it looks from the inside exactly like giving up. He does not lay
the boy on the stone (surrender). He does not leave him in the cage (keeping). He
opens it — and lets him go, freely — and Lunal, watching the child she saved cry in
the cage she built to save him, is the one who finally sets the mask down and lets
go with him. Not defeated. *Joined.* Two answers that both failed, becoming the
third at the last possible moment.

That release **is** the last chord — a voice given freely instead of surrendered,
the one thing the song could never receive and so the one thing that ends it. The
unresolved note resolves. The composition ends, and the world with it — not
destroyed, but at last permitted to rest. The rule dies with the song that made it.
The cage opens because there is no longer a song to keep the boy *from.*

The three lines the game actually says:

> *The hall falls silent. A mask clatters where the huntress stood.*
> *Then — not silence. Rain, far above. And under it, small: a laugh.*
> *Nari. Found. The chorus rests.*

Personal and cosmic in the same chord: the note landing *is* Nari freed — from the
rule that condemned him and the cage that "saved" him, both at once. Rain far above
and a small laugh: the surface, air, a child. **Found** does not mean un-drowned;
it means the family at rest, in a world that finally stopped holding everyone open.
Whether Lunal is there in the quiet, or only her mask, is left exactly as ambiguous
as it should be — but the story knows: she was the second hand on the cage door
when it opened. The world stays open afterward — the echoes remain findable — so the
ending returns Mir to the drowned world rather than hard-stopping: rest, not
erasure.

## 10. Open story threads (design)

- **The Lunal confrontation (the finale's missing half).** The Release canon makes
  Mir's climactic choice a *conscious* refusal of his partner, and the ending a
  shared letting-go. The current build stages that with the mask-clatter (PRD §8.7)
  and a Conductor-only fight; the recognition beat at the Stage threshold and any
  playable Lunal encounter are not yet built. A confrontation with Lunal — a mother,
  not a monster, offering the cage as mercy — is the single highest-value story
  build remaining, and the hardest to write kindly. (Build status: interim
  mask-clatter shipped; recognition beat and Lunal encounter not built.)
- **The Scar's 20-node arc.** The Scar carries 11 of the 20 fight-nodes but draws a
  single fight pool, so the long middle plays as one enemy repeated. §6.4 defines the
  intended three sub-stretches (Fresh Trail → False Trails → Den's Mouth); wiring
  distinct foes/beats/echoes to them is the next content pass, and it is where the
  "keeper is a person, not a beast" reveal should build.
- **The litho / the rite as a set-piece.** The obelisk opening and the litho
  speaking is currently told only in echoes and staging. A scripted opening beat in
  the Fold — the rite Mir is fleeing, shown once, and the two parents splitting over
  it — is the strongest way to seat the premise before the threshold.

## 11. Tone & naming

**Tone.** Beautiful-grim. Surreal, poetic, melancholy dread — Blasphemous /
Hollow-Knight register, not splatter. The horror is in the *images* (a stone baby
that speaks a death, an idol with no hands that rules by yours, melting clocks, a
cage with padded bars and no inside door, a trail of small prints walking the wrong
way) — rendered as gorgeous moody painterly 2D. The distinctive thing this story
owns, and must protect, is that its monster is a mother who is *right,* and its
victory is a letting-go, not a kill. Minimal text; the world speaks through place,
sprite, and song.

**Naming.**

- **Title:** *The Drowned Chorus*
- **Tagline:** *a rhythm of rust and tide*
- **Soundtrack:** six real recorded tracks by the band **Inhalants** (PRD §11.2) —
  *Sunshine Sally*, *Deereater*, *Glassriff*, *John's Anus*, *Truckers for Christ*,
  *Quotience*. (These titles were briefly hero names in a retired draft; they are
  songs, not characters.)
- Prior codename "Project Meterfall" is retired (kept only in git history and older
  PRD revision entries).
