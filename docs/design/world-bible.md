# World & Story Bible — *The Drowned Chorus*

Status: **canonical draft** as of 2026-08-21 (the Harrow revision). Supersedes
the Keeping revision of the same day, and the 2026-07-24 Rite revision.

**What changed and why.** This canon was originally reverse-engineered from the
Inhalants setlist, which left the entire story **music-coded**: a musician hero,
a musician antagonist, and a cosmology made of music. Every character was a
metaphor about sound, which meant none of them were people. Two revisions have
now stripped that out:

- **The Keeping revision** cut the cosmology (an unresolved note, grief-as-time,
  the obelisk's machinery), collapsed the obelisk and the litho into one thing,
  and moved the finale to Lunal — where the actual stakes were the whole time.
- **This revision** finishes the job. Mir is no longer a guitarist — he is a
  **clock-keeper**, sedentary and unfit, chosen so the climb costs him instead of
  equipping him. The Conductor is gone entirely, replaced by a figure whose grief
  is a *physical object* the player walks through for eleven nodes without
  knowing it. And the rhythm mechanic is no longer justified in-fiction at all.

The rule that produced all three fixes, worth keeping: **an abstraction is not an
image.** "He rehearses the same passage forever" cannot be seen, so it cannot
land. A room with padded bars and no door on the inside can be seen in one
second, and it lands in one second. Every remaining piece of this story has to
survive that test.

## 0. How the story is told (the doctrine)

The old rule was "**found, not told**," applied to everything. That was wrong for
the main line and right for everything else. The split, now explicit:

**The spine is TOLD.** Mir's story — the rule, the flight, the taking, the
search, the offer, the choice — is delivered directly and without coyness:
scripted scenes at the seven turning points (§9), NPCs who talk to him and change what
they say as the story moves, and a climax that is an argument in words before it
is a fight. If the player can miss it, it isn't the spine. A player who walks the
critical path and touches nothing optional should finish this game gutted.

**The world is FOUND.** Everything that is not Mir's story is staged physically
and never narrated: dozens of complete little tragedies that happened here before
he walked through, told in wreckage, arrangement, and absence. No pop-ups, no
journals, no lore dumps. The catalog is [`side-stories.md`](side-stories.md).

**Corollary — what we do NOT do.** No narrator explaining the world's rules. No
codex. No character stating the theme out loud (Lunal comes closest, in §7, and
she is *arguing*, not explaining). Nothing in this bible that isn't dramatized is
canon that anyone needs to know.

## 1. Logline

In a drowned town where nothing has changed in living memory, a stone idol
sentences the one child who was never entered in its ledger — so his father
carries him up toward the surface, and finds at the top that the person who took
his son was the boy's own mother, who has built him somewhere he will be safe
forever, and who is not wrong.

---

## 2. The Fold — what the player sees, and what is never explained

At the bottom of a lightless sea lies **the Fold**, a gothic town built in the
silt around a massive **obelisk**. Its people woke there. None of them remember
arriving, none remember drowning, and none find that strange, because it happened
to everyone and there is no one to compare notes with.

Four facts. The game states all four plainly and explains none of them, ever:

- **They did not raise the obelisk.** It was there.
- **Once a year, the obelisk opens and hands down a rule.** A small stone idol
  with an infant's face and no breath behind it comes out of the stone, speaks
  one new commandment into the way of living, and goes back in. The Fold obeys.
  It has always obeyed. No one can recall a year without a rule, or a time before
  the rules, or who they were then.
- **The deep is owed a voice.** Most rules are small — about lamps, or doorways,
  or which way to face. Every so often one names a person, and that person walks
  out past the last prayer-ring and does not come back, and the Fold calls it a
  debt paid.
- **Nothing in the Fold grows.** Bread does not stale. Wounds do not close. Rope
  does not fray. The plaza clocks have hands and no agreement. And the children
  are the age they have always been. Nari has been three years old for longer
  than his father can account for, and so has every other child in town, and
  nobody says so out loud, because saying it out loud would mean asking how long.

That last one is the horror and the engine. **The Fold is a place where nothing
is at risk because nothing is in motion.** The people call that peace. It is
exactly what Lunal wants to give her son forever, and exactly what Mir is willing
to lose him to escape.

*(Design note: resist every urge to answer "why." Why the obelisk, why the tithe,
why the stillness — the game does not know, and it is not withholding a twist.
The images must behave consistently; they do not need a mechanism.)*

## 3. The rhythm (a design note, not a story element)

**The beat is not explained in-fiction, and no character ever remarks on it.**

This was the last piece of music-coding and it goes the same way as the rest. The
world simply moves on a beat — enemies telegraph on it, hazards pulse on it, the
tide answers it — the way a world simply has gravity. Rhythm games do not owe
anyone a reason, and every attempt to provide one here has made the story worse:
first it was a cosmic composition, then it was the protagonist's job. Both were
the tail wagging the dog.

The meters keep their mechanical identity and lose their thematic pretension:
**Focus** is a held breath, **Groove** is nerve, and the forecast lane shows the
next bars. None of them mean anything about the world, and the story is not
allowed to lean on them.

*(Open: "Sightread" is a music term attached to a shipped settings row. Renaming
it to something plain — "Foresight" — is cheap and probably right. §13.)*

## 4. The rule that starts it

**Mir**, his partner **Lunal**, and their son **Nari** live in the Fold, pray in
the Fold, and are unremarkable in it.

The Fold enters its children in the obelisk's **ledger** — a naming, done in the
plaza, the year a child is judged old enough. It is the town's one real ceremony,
it is not optional, and Mir and Lunal have quietly not done it. Three times the
Cantor has raised it, and three times they have found a reason — he's small for
it, he's ill that week, next year. Neither has ever said the real reason out loud
to the other, because the real reason is superstitious and shameful and true:
**they did not want their son written down in that thing.**

Then the obelisk opens, and this year's rule is:

> *The unnamed voice is owed to the deep.*

General on its face. It could mean anyone. There is exactly one unnamed voice in
the Fold, and it is three years old, and it has been three years old for a very
long time.

**Their love is what marked him.** The one protective thing they did is the thing
that selected him. Both parents understand this within a second, neither ever
says it, and it is underneath every choice either of them makes for the rest of
the game. It is why Mir cannot stop. It is why Lunal cannot be reasoned with.

## 5. Three answers

### Mir — *up*

A **clock-keeper.** He maintains the Fold's clocks: the plaza faces, the
tide-bells, the little bench-work brought to him in cloth by neighbours. He sits
in a small warm room with tiny tools and a lens, hunched over mechanisms, and he
has done it every day for as long as he can account for.

**The clocks do not work.** Not one of them in the whole town keeps time or
agrees with any other. He knows this. He winds them anyway, cleans them, adjusts
them, hands them back, and takes the next one — and the Fold lets him, and pays
him, and nobody has ever once said the obvious thing out loud, because his job is
the town's whole sickness in miniature: **maintaining the fiction that time is
passing.**

Which means his want is not a metaphor bolted on later. **He spends every day
trying to make time move inside a box, and failing, and going home to a son who
is not getting any older.** He is the one person in the Fold with a daily,
physical, losing relationship with the idea that things should change. That is
why "I want my son to get older" comes out of him and out of nobody else in town.

And it is why he is **catastrophically the wrong man for this.** He is sedentary,
soft-handed, indoor, unfit, and forty. He has never climbed anything. He has fine
motor control and no stamina whatsoever, and every physical thing the game asks
of him is a thing he has no business doing. He nearly kills them both on the
Shelf — twice — and Lunal is not exaggerating when she says so at the Keep. She
watched.

**His only real competence is that he does not stop**, which is not a skill, and
which is the exact thing the Harrow also has, and which is why they are the same
man thirty years apart.

**The one thing he takes with him.** A clock-keeper's turning tool — small,
fine, precise, and useless for absolutely everything on the climb. He carries it
the whole game for no reason he could defend. See §8.

His answer is **up**. Not because the surface is safe — he has no idea what it
is, and is wrong about it in every particular — but because up is where the rule
does not point, and because of the thing he has never been able to say in the
Fold: **he wants his son to get older.** He wants Nari heavier next year than
this year. He wants to be argued with, eventually. Under the panic about the rule
is a much older panic he has never had a word for.

He is wrong about nearly everything here — the surface, the taking, who he is
hunting — and right about the only thing that matters, which is that a life has
to be allowed to move.

### Lunal — *keep*

Nari's mother. She did not argue the rule either. She asked a different question:
*where can a thing be put that the rule cannot reach it?*

She thinks Mir's answer is a fantasy, and she is largely correct. You cannot
outrun a town's law by walking. The surface is not a sanctuary; it is a waste
that will kill a child in a week. And a man carrying a toddler up a cliff is a
man who will drop him — she watched him nearly do it twice. She has thought about
this more carefully than he has, which is what makes her terrifying.

Her answer is **to keep him.** Find the one place the rule cannot enter, put him
inside it, hold it shut. Safe, whole, fed, exactly as he is, forever — and
*forever* is not a figure of speech to a woman from a town where nothing grows.
It is a known, observed, achievable condition. She is not proposing anything
unnatural. She is proposing the Fold, refined, with the tithe removed and herself
at the door.

She is never to be filed as a monster. She does not hunt to kill; she hunts to
keep. And the reason her horror lands is that **her plan works.** At the end of
it Nari is alive, fed, unharmed, and three years old.

### The Harrow — *what Mir becomes*

**Replaces the Conductor entirely.** No baton, no orchestra, no rehearsal, no
music of any kind.

He is a parent who climbed this route before Mir did — generations back, the last
person to refuse a rule and leave the Fold. He got further than anyone. He
reached the surface with his child, and lost them, and the game never specifies
how, because it does not matter and he cannot get through the sentence.

What he has done every day since: **he has been searching the same ground.**

That is the reveal, and it re-reads the entire middle of the game. **The Scar is
not a monster-haunted waste. The Scar is one man's search.** The claw-gouges are
tool marks. The scorched rings are where he burned ground off to see it better.
The pits are pits. The "den" is where he sleeps. Eleven fight-nodes of hostile,
ruined, clawed-up country that the player has read as *the surface does not want
you* — all of it is one father turning over a continent, for generations, looking
for a child.

He is love as pursuit continued past the point where it had anywhere to go — what
Mir looks like in thirty years if he reaches the top and finds nothing. And
crucially he is **not a puzzle-grief; he is a body-grief.** He digs. His hands
are ruined. There is nothing elegant about him.

**He is also the one who delivers the reveal.** When Mir finally reaches the den
and demands to know what took his son, the Harrow answers him honestly, and it is
the worst news of Mir's life: *nothing up here took your boy. I would know. I
have turned over every inch of this.* Then he shows him — the pits where he has
laid out every small thing he has dug up, sorted, over years beyond counting.
None of them are Nari. One of them may be his own child, and he cannot tell any
more, and he keeps digging because stopping would mean deciding which one it was.

That conversation is what makes the Sitting-Down (§9, beat 5) possible. Mir walks
out of the Scar with his monster-theory dead and only one possibility left.

**And Lunal knows him.** She crossed the Scar before Mir did, met the same man,
and drew the opposite conclusion. She did not see a cautionary tale; she saw
proof — that pursuit is what destroys a parent, and that the only sane move is to
put the child somewhere and *stop*. He is the reason she is so certain. He is the
best argument she has, and Mir has just spent eleven nodes walking through it.

### Nari

Three years old for an unknown number of years. Never an escort objective, never
an inventory item, never a fail state. A small follower at his father's heel
through the Fold and the climb, then footprints, then a sound behind a door.

He must be *specific* — a real toddler, not a symbol. He walks behind Mir for no
reason except that his father is walking. He has one word he overuses. He is
frightened of the wrong things and unbothered by the right ones. Every minute the
player spends with him early is the loan the rest of the game collects on.

## 6. The climb

| Region | Act | What it is | Foe |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The Fold** | *the rule* | home, and the law that names his son | *none — Nari follows* |
| **The Kelp Shelf** | *the flight* | the climb out, child in tow | salt-wracked drifters |
| **The Breach** | *the taking* | the waterline — where Nari is taken | drifter packs |
| **The Scar** | *the search* | one man's excavation, mistaken for a wasteland | wraiths, then **the Harrow** |
| **The Keep** | *the offer* | a drowned hall, made into a nursery | **Lunal** |

### 6.1 The Fold — *the rule*

No combat. The Fold's violence is procedural, and the fighting starts only once
Mir breaks a law.

This region is the loan. The player learns Nari's walk, meets the neighbours, and
is taught — flatly, by people who think they are describing a comfort — that
nothing here changes and everyone is glad. It should be slow, warm, and slightly
wrong, and the player should like it here.

**The Rite** (scripted, opens the game): the obelisk opens, the stone child
speaks, the plaza understands at once. Then the house: two parents and a sleeping
boy, one packing a bag, one already out the door arranging something she does not
explain. **They never have the argument.** That is the point — they each go do
their own answer without putting it to the other, and the entire game is
downstream of that silence.

**The threshold** (scripted): Mir carries Nari past the last prayer-ring.

### 6.2 The Kelp Shelf — *the flight*

Ship skeletons standing like a dead forest, every hull pointing up, and the
salt-wracked remains of a mining town that dug into something and stayed.

Nari is *with him* here, and this region exists to make that hurt later. Small
prints beside big ones. The boy has to be carried over the bad gaps by a man with
no business carrying anything anywhere — and this is where the two near-drops
happen, both of them the player's own hands on the controller, both of them
recoverable, neither of them remarked on. Lunal brings them up at the Keep and
she is not exaggerating.

He talks to Nari constantly because he is terrified. This is the last time they
are together and the player should not know that.

### 6.3 The Breach — *the taking*

The waterline, staged as a carnival that kept performing after the water came.
Mir crosses and takes his first breath of surface air; it is agony and it is the
best moment of his life.

**Then the taking** (scripted). He turns for one measure and the boy is gone. No
blood. No struggle worth the name. Nari did not cry out.

Mir reads: ambush. The ground reads otherwise, and the player gets every piece —
the scuffle is two feet, then one, then none; a second adult set of prints reaches
the surface *ahead* of theirs; and the boy went quietly because the hands that
lifted him were hands he knew.

Everything after this is after the taking, and Mir spends all of it hunting the
wrong culprit. The player should be ahead of him and should hate it.

### 6.4 The Scar — *the search*

The long middle, and now the region with the best secret in the game.

On the way through, it reads as hostile country: gouged, burned, pitted,
picked-over, with a den at the far end. Mir believes a beast took his son and the
landscape obliges the belief. The player believes it too.

The tracking progression runs in the ground — Nari's gait is learnable (paired
stride, heel dot, faint right-foot drag) across three stretches: **the fresh
trail** (clear prints, hope with teeth), **the false trails** (pilgrim strides,
den-thing gaits, decoys that double back), and **the den's mouth** (all tracks
lead one way, none lead back). And underneath it the whole time, the evidence
rotting his monster-story from below: no blood anywhere, prints set down gently
one at a time, and beside the small ones always a woman's stride — *not chasing.
Leading.*

**Then the den, and the man in it,** and the ground stops being a wasteland and
becomes a search (§5). Everything the player crossed to get here means something
else on the walk back out.

**The Oasis** *(optional, off the road)*: one pocket of living green — warm moss,
a clear spring, and visible growth, new shoots, something in bud. The only place
in the game where anything is in motion. No reward but itself, and only a player
who has stopped hurrying will ever stand in it.

### 6.5 The Keep — *the offer*

A drowned concert hall, taken and repurposed. Lunal did not fight anyone for it;
she arrived first and found it empty, which is why she chose it.

The hall's own dead are dressing, not characters — an orchestra that drowned
mid-performance, stands and chairs and stopped clocks. Beautiful, and nobody's
story.

Built on the stage, where an orchestra used to sit: **a room.** A good one. Warm,
lit, stocked, comfortable. Padded bars, and no door on the inside.

Lunal is not hiding. She has been waiting. She left the trail; she wanted him to
come; she has wanted, this entire time, for him to walk in, see it, and **agree.**

## 7. The climax — the argument, then the fight

A conversation before it is a fight, and the conversation is the point.

She shows him the boy: alive, fed, unhurt, three years old, entirely himself. She
tells him the truth — the Fold would have killed him, the surface would have
killed him in a week, and Mir nearly dropped him twice on the Shelf and she
watched. **And she has her closing argument ready, and he has just walked through
it:** *you met him. The one in the Scar. That is what you are. That is where this
ends for you, and I am not going to let our son be what he is still digging for.*

Then the offer, which is not "leave" but **"stay."** Both of them. Here. A
family, kept, in the one room in the world nothing can get into.

Mir refuses, and has to say why, and it is the hardest thing in the game to say:
**he wants his son to be able to be lost.** Heavier next year. Sick, then better,
then sick again. Argued with at fifteen. And eventually — the thing that cannot
happen to anyone from the Fold — he wants to die first.

Lunal's last argument is the true one:

> *"You're choosing a boy who can die. Say it plainly. Say that's what you came
> all this way to give him."*

And he says yes.

**Then the fight.** She does not try to kill him; she tries to *stop* him, and
the mechanics must read that way — holds, blocks, disarms, her body between him
and the room. Three phases are three arguments, each after she has already lost
the previous one. She is fighting a man she loves, in front of their son, to
prevent what she sincerely believes is his murder.

## 8. The ending

He gets past her. And then — **he does not break the cage open.**

The lock is on the outside, and it is a good one, and Mir sits down in front of
it with the small turning tool he has carried since the Fold and picks it, the
way he has opened a thousand mechanisms at a bench in a warm room, with hands
that are now wrecked. It takes a while. Lunal is behind him on the floor and does
not stop him. Nari is on the other side of the bars watching a stranger work.

**This is the whole reason he has a sedentary trade.** The climb never once
rewarded it. It is not a hidden strength and it never saved anybody. It is
simply the thing he was doing while nothing changed — and it turns out to be what
opens the only door that mattered. A man whose life's work was fine, patient,
pointless mechanism, finally with something on the other side of one.

**Nari does not know him at once.** He has been kept a while. That beat is the
price of the climb and it should be allowed to be ugly for a few seconds.

Lunal is not killed — killing her lets the player off. She is left in the hall
with the empty room, and the last thing she says to his back is not a curse; it
is the same argument, one more time, because she will never be finished with it.

They go up. It rains.

> *He is heavier than he was. That is the first miracle.*
>
> *He has never seen rain. He puts out a hand, and pulls it back, and laughs.*
>
> *And then he asks me where we're going.*
>
> *He has never asked that before. Nothing where we come from was ever going
> anywhere.*
>
> *I don't know, Nari. I don't know. Isn't it good?*

No villain was defeated and nothing cosmic resolved. A father chose motion over
safety on his son's behalf, without his son's consent, against the boy's mother,
and he may have chosen wrong. **A child asking where they are going is the entire
payoff** — a question about the future, from a boy who has never had one.

The world stays open afterward. The Fold is still down there, still owed a voice
next year.

## 9. What the game says out loud (the told spine)

| # | Beat | Where | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The Rite** | Fold, on new game | Obelisk, stone child, rule. Establishes the ledger and the stillness. |
| 2 | **The House** | Fold, pre-threshold | Two parents, one sleeping boy, two plans, no conversation. |
| 3 | **The Threshold** | Leaving the Fold | The first heretic in living memory. Nari follows. |
| 4 | **The Taking** | First step onto the surface | One measure. Gone. No blood. He was not afraid. |
| 5 | **The Den** | End of the Scar | The Harrow. The pits. *Nothing up here took your boy.* |
| 6 | **The Sitting-Down** | Leaving the Scar | Alone, with his own evidence, finally reading it. No fight music for a while. |

Plus the ending (§8). Beats 5 and 6 have no equivalent in the old canon and are
the most important additions: the retired story had the reveal happen *to the
player* and never to Mir, which is why its finale had nothing to land on.

## 10. NPCs — the told layer, continued

Every significant NPC has beats keyed to story flags (`leftFold`, `nariLost`,
per-node clears), so the same person says different things before Mir leaves,
after he leaves, and after the taking.

- **The Cantor** — the town's faith, entirely sincere, entirely awful. He is the
  one who kept asking about the ledger. Never write him as a hypocrite; his grief
  for Mir is real, which is worse.
- **Meret** — a mother who lost a child to a rule and folded. What Lunal refused
  to become. Her scene after the taking is the game's cruellest kindness.
- **The Ledger-Keeper** — writes the names. His hands shake now. He has entered
  every child in the Fold and cannot remember doing most of it.
- **A neighbour who saw Lunal go** — plants, early and missably, that she went out
  the night the rule was read and came back with mud on her boots from somewhere
  there is no mud.

## 11. Tone

Beautiful-grim, and **warm where it counts.** The Fold has to be genuinely lovely
or the rest is just misery: neighbours who like each other, a boy who is funny,
bread that never goes stale.

The horror is in images: a stone baby that speaks a death sentence, clocks that
do not agree, a room with padded bars, a shelf of small things arranged by size,
a field of pits with a man still digging, a mother who is right.

**The hands.** The one visual arc that runs the whole game and needs no dialogue.
Mir starts with a clock-keeper's hands — clean, soft, precise. The Harrow's, when
Mir finally sees them at the den, are ruined past use: that is what pursuit does
to a body over generations. By the Keep, Mir's are on their way there. Whatever
the art register ends up being, portraits and idle poses should be built to carry
this, because it is the entire character in one place the player can see.

**Naming.** Tagline: *a rhythm of rust and tide*. Soundtrack: six recorded tracks
by **Inhalants** — *Sunshine Sally*, *Deereater*, *Glassriff*, *John's Anus*,
*Truckers for Christ*, *Quotience*. The band is the soundtrack's real-world
attribution and has no in-fiction presence.

**Title — open (§13).** *The Drowned Chorus* is a music title on a story that no
longer has music in it. It is on the wordmark and it is a good phrase; it is also
the last piece of setlist DNA in the project.

## 12. What the fight design has to deliver (open — story-first)

1. **The Harrow is not a boss you beat for loot.** Whether he is fought at all is
   open — a fight that ends in a conversation, a conversation that becomes a
   fight, or no fight. What is fixed: the player leaves that den with Mir's
   monster-theory dead.
2. **Lunal fights to stop, not to kill.** Holds, walls, disarms, positioning
   between Mir and the room. A phase that reads as "trying to win" is a bug.
3. **Three phases are three arguments**, each after she has already lost the
   previous one. Escalation is desperation, not power.
4. **Nari is visible during the final fight** — in the room, watching his parents.
   Non-interactive. The strongest staging decision available; build the fight
   around it.
5. **The player must be able to hesitate.** Refusing to swing at her for a while
   should be possible and should be noticed.

## 13. Open threads

- **The title.** See §11. Options: keep it and let "chorus" mean the town's one
  unchanging hymn (which is still music-coded, honestly), or re-title and re-letter
  the wordmark. Not urgent, not free.
- **"Sightread."** Music jargon on a shipped settings row. Rename to "Foresight."
- **The Scar's length.** Eleven of twenty nodes drawing one foe pool, so the long
  middle plays as one enemy repeated. §6.4's three stretches want distinct foes,
  distinct ground, and distinct staging — and now they also want to escalate the
  *digging* evidence, so the den lands.
- **How much Nari talks.** Specific enough to miss. Too much and he is a mascot;
  too little and he is a prop. Err toward few words, heavily repeated.
- **Whether Lunal survives in the player's sight.** Currently left alive, no
  confirmation, no epilogue. Tempting to resolve. Don't.
- **The Harrow's child.** Never specified — not the loss, not the name, not the
  ending. He cannot finish the sentence and neither should the game.
