# World & Story Bible — *The Drowned Chorus*

Status: **canonical** as of 2026-07-30 (the **Release revision**, hardened by a
three-lens adversarial review — logic, character, originality). This tightens the
Rite canon to a single engine — *the chorus cannot take, only receive* — and, more
importantly, makes the one thing this story owns its center: **the antagonist is a
mother who is right, and the victory is a letting-go so costly it looks like
surrender.** It supersedes the "Project Meterfall" codename (the game is **The
Drowned Chorus**).

The world is built from the recorded setlist of the band **Inhalants** — the
game's world *is* those songs, sunk and given a place. The playable character is
**Mir** alone (PRD v10.0); the *story* is a family of three: Mir, his partner
**Lunal**, and their toddler son **Nari**.

**How the story is told.** Almost none of what follows is ever spoken. The game
narrates through *place* (§7), *ground* (the footprint trail, §6.4), ~55 found
**echoes** (§8), and three lines at the very end (§9). This bible is the canon
*beneath* all of that. Two standing rules govern everything below:

1. **Found, not told.** If a beat can be paraphrased as "the moral is X," it is
   cut or buried. The player infers; the game does not lecture.
2. **Leave slack.** Not everything in this world decodes to the theme. A world
   where every object is a facet of one idea reads as a puzzle-box, not a place —
   so some images, echoes, and rooms mean *nothing*, and are there only to be
   strange, ugly, funny, or ordinary. The elegiac notes only land if something
   around them is mundane. **Where this document names texture-with-no-meaning,
   protect it — do not "improve" it into symbolism.**

---

## 1. Logline

A drowned father runs his condemned son up out of a town that has lawfully sworn
the boy to the deep — and finds, at the top of the world, that the one who took
his child is the boy's own mother, who is not wrong; and that the only way to free
Nari is the one thing worse than losing him to her, or to the song.

## 2. Premise — the Rite, and the flight from it

At the bottom of a lightless sea lies **the Fold**, a gothic town that prays to a
massive **obelisk**. They did not raise it; they woke on the ocean floor and it
was already there, already listening. Time is broken here — but not stopped. **It
turns and never resolves:** years pass, children are born and grow, but nothing
*ends* — nothing heals, nothing rots, nothing rests, nothing is ever allowed to be
over. The whole drowned realm hums one endless composition, **the drowned
chorus**, and the obelisk is that chorus made monument.

The Fold is *governed* by it. **Once a year the obelisk opens, and the litho comes
out**: a small, baby-shaped stone idol, an infant's face with no breath behind it,
that speaks one new **rule**. And the Fold performs it — not because it is forced,
but because the obelisk has no hands and cannot force anything (§3); the rule only
takes hold when the people *give* it their obedience, freely, at the opening. They
have given it, year over year, so long that giving is all they remember how to do.

**Mir**, **Lunal**, and their toddler **Nari** live there, and pray there, like
everyone. Then the obelisk opens and the litho speaks this year's rule: *the
unnamed voice is owed to the deep.* General on its face — but the Fold knows at
once whose small voice it means. And here is the mercy that is also the trap: the
rule cannot be *seized*. It must be *performed* — the boy given up, freely, at the
**next** opening, a year away. The town will not lay a hand on Nari. It will
simply wait, certain, for his parents to bring him to the stone, because everyone
always has.

That year is the game. Mir and Lunal both mean to save him, and cannot agree how,
and the disagreement is the whole story (§5). Mir commits the only heresy left: he
takes his son and **runs** — up the drowned Shelf, across the Breach where the
world meets the waterline, out onto the surface — because up is the one direction
the rule does not point. (This is not "the ones who walk away." Omelas leaves the
suffering child behind; Mir runs *carrying* him. That inversion is the story's
spine — keep it sharp, and prune any beat that reads as unmodified Lottery/Omelas.)
He is not climbing toward safety. He is outrunning a deadline. And at the top, the
thing that finally takes Nari is not a monster of the surface. It is the boy's own
mother, saving him her way.

## 3. The one law — the chorus cannot take, only receive

*(The cosmology. Never stated outright in-game; it is the physics the images obey.
Note the slack rule above: this law explains much of the world, but not all of
it, and it should not.)*

Long ago — or outside of time, which here is the same — something sang a note that
did not resolve: the first phrase of a composition with no ending. Because the
chord never lands, nothing else can end either; that is why nothing heals and the
clocks in the deep are all **stopped** — each one halted at the exact instant its
owner gave up (not melting; *stopped* — the melting-clock image is retired, it was
borrowed). The unresolved note is a hunger, and it grows the only way it can: **it
cannot seize a voice. It can only accept one that is surrendered to it.** Five
things fall out of that, and they are the load-bearing beams:

- **Surrender is the mercy and the theft in one gesture.** Hand the song your grief
  and it soothes you; the handing-over is what presses you into the sound. The
  drowned wake in the Fold with no memory of drowning because that memory was the
  first thing they gave.
- **An idol with no hands rules through the town's.** The obelisk cannot drown the
  Fold; it must be fed, so it *legislates*, and every rule is a fresh surrender the
  people perform themselves. It cannot take Nari. It can only wait for him to be
  brought.
- **Nari cannot be taken, only given — and only a *particular* giving will do.**
  He is an **unwritten voice**: a child born in the sheltered deep who has
  surrendered nothing, so the song has no hold on him to pull. Crucially, what the
  song wants is not merely Nari handed over — it is Nari handed over *still bound to
  the grief around him*, a voice thick with unresolved loss, because **that** is
  the kind of voice the note can feed on. This is why the rule exists: to
  engineer a grieving surrender.
- **To resist is to keep your grief unsurrendered.** A voice that refuses to give —
  a grief held, not handed over — beats against the note and will not lie flat.
  That refusal *is* Mir, and it is the combat (§4). And the song reaches only for
  what refuses it: it claws at Mir the whole climb because he fights it, and it let
  Lunal walk the same road untouched because she did not fight — she *bargained*
  (§5, §6.5).
- **Release is not surrender, and the difference is the whole game.** Surrender
  hands the song a voice still knotted with grief — fresh tension, which the note
  holds and feeds on, and the song grows louder. **Release** hands over a voice
  with the grief *let go* — nothing unresolved left for the note to catch — and a
  voice with nothing to hold is the one thing that lets the chord finally land.
  From the inside the two acts are indistinguishable; that confusion is why the
  song has run forever, because every soul who ever tried to end it clutched its
  grief as it let go, and surrendered instead of releasing, and only made the note
  louder. To release is to hand over the thing you love *and* the grief of losing
  it, both, freely — which feels exactly like giving up, and is the hardest thing
  in the world.

### The three answers, and the two false ones made into objects

Loss has three answers here, and the world hands the player the two wrong ones as
objects it meets over and over:

- **The obelisk — surrender.** It takes a grief and dissolves it into the song.
  Give it away, be numbed, sink. It is also the **save-and-rest point**, the Fold's
  prayer-stones scattered up every region (PRD §8.8), and that is deliberate: at
  each one Mir lets the song take the road's weariness and *clenches around the one
  thing he will never lay down.* Every save is his character in one gesture — **I'll
  give you the weariness. Not him** — which is why resting never claims him. (This
  is the one genuinely diegetic mechanic already in the game. §4 exists to make the
  combat as diegetic as the save.)
- **The cage — keeping.** Lunal's answer (§5): it takes a grief and stops it dead,
  held forever, unchanged. The obelisk numbs pain by dissolving it; the cage numbs
  it by freezing it. Both refuse to let the loss *move.*
- **Release** — the third answer — has no object, because it cannot be built out of
  the world's materials. It is the only thing that ends anything, and the only
  thing no one down here can tell apart from despair.

## 4. The rhythm is diegetic — including the part you feel in your hands

The combat mechanic is the story: a battle is a passage of the chorus, every
ability a phrase sung into it. The foes are the song made flesh — voices it already
owns, reaching to pull Mir under because he refuses to be pulled. Hitting the beat
(PRD §8.3) is refusing to hand the phrase over; missing gives it up.

- **Focus** — a breath held for a hard line, spent on specials.
- **Groove** — the chorus turning in Mir's favour; spent whole on an **ultimate**.
- **Sightread** — seeing the next bars coming (PRD §8.4's forecast assist).

**The core rule the theme demands (design intent, §10).** Today the kit is a
conventional light/heavy/special/ultimate/dash/parry, with music words on top —
narrative paint. The one law says it should go deeper: *Mir can never take.* No
lifesteal, no draining, no stealing a foe's meter. His offense is the **refused
beat** — the song offers him a phrase and damage is what happens when he refuses to
accept it (the on-beat parry/counter is the truest attack in the kit). And a missed
beat is not merely lost, it is *given*: a sliver of Groove passes to the enemy,
because a phrase you fail to refuse is a phrase you hand over. *You cannot take;
you can only refuse, or give.* Until the hands feel that, the conceit is
decoration.

## 5. The family — one doomed child, three answers

The rule condemns Nari. Everyone who loves him answers differently, and none of
the answers is villainous at root — they are three kinds of love. The old framing
called this "run vs. keep"; that was wrong, because Lunal climbs too. The real axis
is **keep vs. release**, and Mir and Lunal stand on the *same* side of it for the
entire game — both refuse to lose him — until the very top, where only one of them
learns the second half.

### Mir — love as refusal

A guitarist, and a father. When the litho named his son, Mir did not argue the
rule; he started packing. He is not chosen and not strong — he is only the one soul
in the Fold who would rather leave (heresy) than perform the giving (obedience). He
can sing back where the drowned cannot because he never finished drowning: his
refusal to let go is an unresolved chord of his own. He is *wrong about almost
everything* — what the surface is, what took his son — and right about the one
thing that matters until it becomes the one thing that damns him: **you do not give
the boy up.** His whole climb, his whole kit, his every save-point is a grip. What
he cannot imagine, and must, is that the hand that saves Nari is the one that
opens — and that opening it is not the surrender he has spent his life refusing.

**His turn has to be played, not stated (§10).** Late, at the Stage, Mir must
*try* to take Nari back the only way he knows — seize him, hold him — and it must
**fail**, because a boy in a keeper's cage cannot be pulled free by a tighter grip
(§9). The final opening of his hand has to be a break, not a correct menu choice.

### Lunal — love as keeping, and she is not wrong

Nari's mother, Mir's partner. She did not argue the rule either; she asked where a
thing could be hidden that the song could not find. She believes running is a
fantasy, and **the game proves her right**: you cannot outclimb the obelisk, and
the surface is not freedom, only a wider grave (the Scar, §6.4, is her argument
made into eleven fight-nodes). So she chose the one answer she thought could work —
**keep him.** Carry him where the rule and the song can't reach, and hold him
there, exactly as he is, forever: never given, never grown, never lost.

She is the antagonist, and she must never read as a monster, which means the bible
owes her what it has withheld — **interior.** Give her, on screen, at least these:
a memory of Nari that is small and specific and not about the plot; a hesitation at
the cage door *before* she closes it; and the thing she gave up to build her
fortress (she bargained her own way to the Stage, §6.5 — name the cost). And the
hardest, truest beat: **she has been living beside the crying.** Nari cries in the
cage (§7), and Lunal stays, and the horror is not that she cannot hear it — it is
that she can, and has decided a caged, crying, *existing* son is better than a
freed and vanished one. That is a defensible, unbearable position, and the story's
job is to make the player feel its pull, not to refute it. *"She doesn't hunt to
kill. She hunts to keep."*

At the Breach she takes Nari while Mir's back is turned for a measure — and the boy
does not cry out, because the hands that lift him are his mother's. That fact (§6.3,
§8) is the most devastating in the game and must be a **mandatory** beat, not
optional wall-text. Her cage is one a mother builds: padded bars, and **no door on
the inside** — which is also the mechanism of the ending (§9). She is right about
the danger and wrong about the cost, and she is granted the dignity of the
distinction.

### Nari — a child, not a device

Mir and Lunal's toddler, and the one thing the chorus does not own. He is the
*reason*, and the story must resist reducing him to a MacGuffin whose value is
mechanical. Yes, his voice matters to the song (below) — but the game owes at least
one beat that is only about **him**: his face at the cage bars, his fear, the
moment he recognizes his father and it changes nothing about whether Mir can free
him. Reach him as a person, once, or the ending is a physics puzzle with a child
glued to it.

*(On his voice: an unwritten, uneaten voice is one the song cannot digest, so
Nari's crying **destabilizes** the chorus — it makes the note falter and crack. But
a cracked note is not a resolved one: the cry can shake the song, never end it.
Only a voice *released* lands the chord. This is why keeping Nari caged and crying
is safe for the song and for his keepers — a destabilizer is not a resolution — and
it is what the Conductor's whole fortress is built to preserve. See §9; do not let
"the cry ends the song" and "the release ends the song" both be true — only the
release does.)*

### The Conductor — keeping, grown terminal; and a hostage

The boss at the far end of the world (PRD §8.7): gaunt, black-coated, a stopped
clock where a heart should tick, a baton over a drowned orchestra. He is what Lunal
becomes and what Mir is one refusal from — a keeper so old he has fused to the song
he refused to let end. Landing the chord unmakes him, so he conducts to survive.
Lunal did not fight him; she offered him what a keeper cannot refuse — **a reason to
keep going, and a thing worth keeping** — and took up behind his baton, so that his
endless rehearsal walls off the one room the rule cannot enter. And here is why he
houses the one voice that can crack his song: **while Nari is *kept*, he cannot be
*released*, and only release ends anything.** A caged, crying, un-given child is not
the song's danger — it is the song's **insurance**, the proof that the one voice
that could resolve the note is safely held where no one can let it go. Break the
Conductor and you do not free Nari; you only tear down the wall, exposing the cage
for the one measure it takes to do the thing the whole world was built to prevent.
His three phases are the song straining: a stable **4/4** fracturing to **3/4**,
then a desperate **5/4 → 7/8 → 4/4 → 3/4**, faltering each time the small one
cries, then wound tighter and louder.

## 6. The Ascent — regions as movements, and the shape of the flight

One continuous climb, **20 fight-nodes** across five regions. Combat begins only
once Mir leaves the Fold — the Fold's violence is a *law*, not a brawl.

| Region | Movement | What it is | Staged as | Foe |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **The Fold** | *the rule* | home, and the law that condemns the boy | drowned harvest village + the rite | *none — Nari still follows* |
| **The Kelp Shelf** | *the flight* | the climb away, child in tow | drowned salt mine | the salt-choked (miners who gave themselves by degrees) |
| **The Breach** | *the taking* | the waterline — where Lunal takes Nari | drowned carnival | the revelers (given to the show) |
| **The Scar** | *the wrong search* | hostile surface; Mir hunts a "monster" | the collector's outer rings | the cast-off (a keeper's discards) |
| **The Stage** | *the truth* | the mother's fortress and the cage | drowned concert hall | **the Conductor** (Lunal behind him) |

### 6.1 The Fold — the rule

Mir wakes on the silt among people who pray to the obelisk and live by its rules.
The one region with no combat: the horror here is bureaucratic. The obelisk opens
(the plaza is built around it; it is the first save-obelisk and the threshold-beat
site). The litho speaks: *the unnamed voice is owed to the deep.* Then the quiet
catastrophe of two parents and a sleeping child — one packing a bag, one testing
locks — the same love splitting, both of them still on the *keep* side of the line,
neither yet knowing that is where the danger is. There is time to run only because
the rule cannot seize, only receive (§2): the town will not chase, it will wait.
Mir's first step out fires the threshold beat once (*"THE FOLD BEHIND YOU / The
chorus begins."*). *(Slack: the Fold's festival junk — a raffle drum still half
full of wooden tokens, a pie-eating banner — means nothing. Leave it meaningless;
it is what makes the obelisk heavy.)*

### 6.2 The Kelp Shelf — the flight

Ship skeletons standing like a dead forest, every wreck pointing the same way, *up.*
Mir carries or leads Nari here — small prints beside his on the masts; the follower
is still with him, and it does not last. This is the region of bargains with weight:
*"rope enough to reach the light, if you don't weigh anything anymore"* — surrender
dressed as ascent. The miners who worked it broke into a gallery that sang back and
gave it a little of themselves to hear it better, and a little more, until they were
salt (the Fold's fate in miniature — surrender by degrees). Mir climbs it badly,
carrying the one weight he will never set down.

### 6.3 The Breach — the taking

The waterline: the one seam where the drowned dark meets air, thin as a footstep,
thin enough that a small thing is *carried* through. Staged as a carnival that kept
performing after the water came. **This is where Lunal takes Nari.** On Mir's first
step onto the surface the loss beat fires once (*"NARI? / He was right behind
you."*): he turns for a measure and the boy is gone, the follower sprite fading, the
fact saved forever. Mir reads an ambush by the surface. The ground tells the truer
story to anyone who studies it — a scuffle that is two feet, then one, then none;
no blood; a second, adult set of prints reaching the air *ahead* of theirs, the
small ones going with them unhurried. **The boy did not cry out.** That is the beat
the whole game turns on, and it is mandatory.

### 6.4 The Scar — the wrong search *(the long middle: 11 nodes)*

The hostile surface, and the heart of the game — but no longer a generic waste. The
Scar is re-conceived as **the outer rings of the collector's den**: from its first
node the land itself is arranged, sorted, *kept* — cairns graded by size, things
set in rows, a wrongness that is not savagery but *tidiness*, closing in as Mir
climbs. Eleven nodes should read as "the keeper is getting nearer," not "the same
monster eleven times." It also, quietly, proves Lunal right: the surface is not
freedom, only a wider grave — and Mir walks a hundred wrong miles through her
argument without knowing it is hers.

The search runs in the ground (the tracking mystery — Nari's gait is learnable:
paired stride, a heel dot, a faint right-foot drag). **Critically, Mir's own echo
voice must not out-run his knowledge:** the player reads, in the prints, *a keeper
who is careful, who leads and does not chase, who sets each print down gently* — but
Mir himself registers only "not a beast; a person; careful." He does not name a
*she*, and does not endorse her argument, until the Stage. The dramatic irony is the
player seeing the shape of the mother before Mir can bear to.

- **Fresh Trail** *(early).* Prints clear, Mir close. Hope with teeth.
- **False Trails** *(mid).* Pilgrims' strides, three-toed den-things, decoys that
  double back. He loses it and finds it. The ground rots his monster-story: no
  blood, prints set gently one at a time, an adult stride always *beside* the small
  ones, leading.
- **The Den's Mouth** *(late).* Tracks all lead in; none lead back. A shelf of
  little things arranged by size, kept not eaten. The player understands what Mir
  cannot yet: the keeper is a person who loves him.

**The Oasis** *(secret, off the road).* One living thing survives the dead surface:
a pocket of warm green, a clear spring. It rhymes with Nari — the unwritten voice,
the one thing the song has not claimed; the one ground it has not drowned. Off the
critical path. Finding it is finding, in miniature, the thing Mir climbs to protect
— *and the exact temptation Lunal felt:* proof one live thing can persist here, and
proof of how badly you would cage it to keep it. **Seed release here and in the
Fold**, so the winning answer is not a stranger at the finale.

### 6.5 The Stage — the truth

A drowned concert plaza and the hall beyond. At the threshold the ground stops
lying to Mir: something only Lunal would leave, the padded bars through the doors,
a stride he has slept beside for years — and he *knows*, and it must be a beat we
**watch**, not a line saying he "walks in already broken." Inside: the cage, the
Conductor at his stand, and behind the baton Lunal — who bargained her way here, not
battled, trading the Conductor a reason to play for the walls of her fortress.

The Stage is the game's real question as a fight, and Mir now knows exactly whom he
answers. Lunal offers him her answer made real — your son, kept, whole, safe,
forever, in the only place the song can't touch — and she offers it as the woman he
loves, who is right about the danger. To "win" is not to prove her wrong; she isn't.
It is to break the wall of keeping (the Conductor), reach the cage, **fail to tear
it open by force** (Mir's grip, the thing that carried him the whole way, cannot
free a kept child — §9), and arrive at the one answer neither parent could reach
while they were still refusing to lose him. It is not a father overruling a mother.
It is two people who spent a whole drowned world on the *keep* side of the line,
learning the other half of love within the same minute — each by a different act
only they can perform (§9).

## 7. The untold stories (canonical arena staging)

Staged pasts the game never narrates (PRD §11.1.1). Never surfaced as text.

- **The Fold (village + rite).** Drowned at its harvest festival — the ring of boats
  was a blessing rite; the water came with the first verse and most didn't run. One
  boat still strains at its mooring: the baker's daughter cut everyone else's loose
  first and hers ran out of rope. Once a year the stone child legislates. *(Slack:
  not every drowned house has a story. Most just drowned.)*
- **The Kelp Shelf (salt mine).** Miners gave a singing gallery a little of
  themselves to hear it better, and became salt. The one statue facing away is the
  foreman, who covered his ears and ran, and calcified mid-stride three steps from
  the lift.
- **The Breach (carnival).** Kept performing after the water came. The champion's
  last match was against something that came up through the ring floor; the ropes
  snapped outward when it left with him. The tipped chairs face away because in the
  end no one could watch.
- **The Scar (the collector's rings).** Everything sorted, kept, arranged by size,
  nothing eaten. Mir reads a monster's larder; the staging contradicts him — the
  care, the gentleness — because it was never a beast. (A smaller keeper is staged
  here too: a room boarded from the inside, where someone locked themselves in to
  transcribe the sound before it finished transcribing them.)
- **The Stage (concert hall).** The orchestra drowned rehearsing the ending. The
  **clocks are all stopped** — each at the instant a player gave up — because the
  Conductor keeps those instants open; the stands' pages are blank because he erases
  every attempt but the one that came closest, the only players who ever got it
  right and whom he could never let leave. The music falters every time the small
  one cries. Behind the baton, a mother watches, and keeps.

## 8. The echoes — the explorable world (PRD §8.8)

~55 hand-placed fragments, one line each, surfaced only when walked to (PRD
§8.8.2). They must obey both standing rules (§0): **found, not told** — no echo
states the moral; if it can be paraphrased as "the lesson is X," it is cut — and
**slack** — a handful mean nothing, and are kept concrete and strange on purpose.
Mir's first-person echoes never out-run his knowledge (§6.4).

- **The Fold** plants the engine before Mir leaves — concrete, never thesis: *"Once
  a year the stone opens and the small idol speaks. Once a year we answer it."* —
  *"A baby's face, and no breath behind it."* — *"The unnamed voice is owed to the
  deep. We all knew whose small voice it meant."* — *"It has no hands. That is why
  it needs ours."* — *"One of us packed a bag. One of us tested the locks. He slept
  between us."* *(Slack echo: "The raffle drum is still half full. Nobody ever drew
  the last token." — means nothing; keep it.)*
- **The Kelp Shelf:** *"We aren't climbing toward anything. We're climbing away."* —
  *"They gave the gallery a little of themselves to hear it better. That's all salt
  is."* — *"Little prints on the mast beside mine."*
- **The Breach:** *"The scuffle is two feet, then one, then none."* — *"A second set
  of prints reached the air before ours."* — *"Whatever lifted him did not frighten
  him. He did not cry out. That is the part I cannot forgive."*
- **The Scar** — the search rotting into dread, Mir's voice never naming her: *"It
  doesn't eat what it takes. It keeps it."* — *"The prints are set down gently. One
  at a time. Whatever this is, it is careful."* — *"An adult stride, always beside
  the small ones. Leading, not chasing. I don't let myself finish the thought."*
- **The Stage** — the truth, still never stated as moral: *"There is no door on the
  inside of the bars."* — *"She stayed. All this time, beside the crying, she
  stayed."* — *"I came all this way to take him back. My hands are the wrong tool
  for this."*

## 9. The ending

Mir breaks the Conductor — the wall of keeping — and the baton comes free of Lunal's
hand. Then the cage, and the trap the whole game has been walking him into: **he
cannot pull Nari out.** His hands, his grip, the refusal that carried him up a
drowned world, are the wrong tool — a kept child cannot be seized free; a cage with
no inside door does not answer to force. He tries. It fails. That failure is his
whole arc landing at once: the thing he is best at cannot save his son.

And the answer is the one no one down here can tell from despair. Nari is drowned —
he has been since before the Fold; everyone there is. So the three answers were
never life and death. They were: **surrender** — give the boy to the song bound in
grief, and he dissolves into the note and is *erased*, feeding it. **Keep** —
Lunal's cage, where he goes on *existing*, whole and crying and unchanging, forever,
still *there*. And **release** — let him go with the grief let go too, freely, which
does not bring him back and does not keep him: it lets him *end*. Rest. Stop. To
choose it is to choose to lose your son for real and forever, gently, over keeping a
caged, crying copy of him — to give up the one thing you have left of him because
holding it is a cage of your own. It is monstrous and it is the only mercy, and it
is why it took the end of the world for anyone to tell it from giving up.

It cannot be done by one of them. The cage is Lunal's, built with no inside door,
and only its keeper can open it — Mir's force never could. And it cannot be opened
into another grip; Mir must be the one who does not snatch the boy back as the bars
part. So it is **two** releases, neither a copy of the other: **Lunal unmakes the
thing she built** — opens her own hands, the one act reserved to her — and **Mir
does not close his.** The mother lets the cage go; the father lets the holding go;
and Nari, given freely and free of the grief that would have bound him, is the one
voice the song could never receive. The note it has held open since before the world
drowned finally has nothing left to catch.

That is the last chord. It lands. The composition ends — the whole of it, not blown
apart but permitted, at last, to be *over*. The rule dies with the song that made
it. Time is allowed to resolve. The three lines the game says:

> *The hall falls silent. A mask is set down where the huntress stood.*
> *Then — not silence. Rain, far above. And under it, small: a laugh.*
> *Nari. Found. The chorus rests.*

Read it honestly: **Found** is not *rescued.* Nari does not grow up on a surface
that never existed to save him. Found is the opposite of *kept* and the opposite of
*eaten* — a child no longer held open in the dark, at rest, and his parents with
him. The rain and the laugh are the sound of a thing finally allowed to be over,
and whether that is a mercy or a grave is the same question the whole game has been
— the story does not blink from it and does not answer it. The mask is *set down*,
not struck off: Lunal chose. She is the second hand on the cage. The world stays
open afterward — the echoes remain — so the ending returns the player to the
drowned world at rest, not to a black screen: release, not erasure.

## 10. Open threads — and the honest gap between this canon and the build

The elegance of this bible is not the achievement; the achievement would be
*staging* the human breaks the cosmology is supposed to produce, and most are not
built yet. In descending order of how much they matter:

- **Lunal, made real, is the whole game.** She is the single original thing here
  (an antagonist who is right; a mother, not a monster). The current build ships the
  *derivative* half — a gaunt clock-hearted Conductor boss — and withholds the
  invention: her recognition beat, her interior beats (§5), a confrontation in her
  own voice, and the two-hand release (§9) are **not built** (the finale is an
  interim mask-clatter, PRD §8.7). Build her before any other content pass. Nothing
  else moves the grade as much.
- **The four unbuilt turning-points.** Mir's failed-seizure and the opening of his
  hand (§9); Lunal opening her own cage (§9); the *watched* recognition at the Stage
  threshold (§6.5); and one beat that is only about Nari as a child (§5). Right now
  these live as prose here and stage-directions there. They are the A-to-A+ gap.
- **The mandatory devastating beat.** *"He did not cry out"* (§6.3) is currently an
  optional echo. Promote it to a beat the player cannot miss.
- **The diegetic combat rule** (§4): *you cannot take, only refuse or give.* A real
  rule, not narration. PRD-side design work.
- **The Scar reskin** (§6.4): re-theme all 11 nodes as the collector's closing rings
  so the long middle reads as dread approaching, and spread more of the 20 nodes
  into the other four biomes so the back half isn't one repeated foe.
- **The rite as a set-piece** (§6.1): show the obelisk opening and the litho
  speaking, and the two parents splitting, once, before the threshold.

## 11. Tone, lineage & naming

**Tone.** Beautiful-grim; Blasphemous / Hollow-Knight register, not splatter. The
horror is in the *images* — a stone baby that speaks a death; an idol with no hands
that rules through the town's; a cage with padded bars and no inside door; a clock
stopped at the instant its owner gave up; a trail of small prints walking the wrong
way. **Protect the images this story invented** (the litho, the handless idol,
save-as-surrender, the stopped clocks, and above all Lunal-the-correct-antagonist)
and **cut the borrowed ones** (melting clocks — retired; generic "monster-strewn
waste" — retired for the collector's rings). The one thing to defend to the death:
the monster is a mother who is right, and the victory is a letting-go that costs
more than the loss. Register: mostly elegiac, but let it rest — one ordinary,
ugly, or wry note per region so the grief has contrast. Minimal text.

**Lineage (know it, to swerve it).** The premise is Shirley Jackson's "The Lottery"
(a community's lawful, general-worded child-sacrifice everyone privately decodes)
fused with Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" (the child suffering so
the world may hold). The swerve is the spine: Omelas is about those who *leave the
child behind*; this is a parent who *runs carrying him*, and an ending about the
one who could not walk away and had to learn to let go instead. The grieving-parent
/ let-go ending is the genre's most-trodden path (Last of Us, Ico, Souls' fade) —
which is exactly why the distinctiveness is loaded onto *keeping*, not letting-go,
and why the release is written to cost what that orthodoxy usually launders. Keep
leading with keeping.

**Naming.**

- **Title:** *The Drowned Chorus* · **Tagline:** *a rhythm of rust and tide*
- **Soundtrack:** six recorded tracks by **Inhalants** (PRD §11.2) — *Sunshine
  Sally*, *Deereater*, *Glassriff*, *John's Anus*, *Truckers for Christ*,
  *Quotience*. (Briefly hero names in a retired draft; they are songs.)
- Codename "Project Meterfall" retired (git history only).
